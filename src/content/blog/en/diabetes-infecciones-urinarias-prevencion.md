# Diabetes and Urinary Infections: Why and How to Prevent

If you live with diabetes, you probably keep an eye on plenty of numbers: your morning glucose, your A1C every few months, maybe your blood pressure. What few people mention is that those numbers also matter for your bladder. When sugar stays high for long stretches, urinary infections become more likely, they reach the kidneys more easily and, most worrying of all, they sometimes give very little warning.

Here the clinic's medical team explains how diabetes and the bladder are connected, which quiet signs deserve your attention and what you can start doing today so an infection doesn't undo the control you work so hard to keep.

## How blood sugar affects the bladder

It isn't bad luck, and it isn't about being careless with hygiene. Your own body chemistry explains why people with diabetes are more prone to these infections:

- **Urine carries extra sugar.** Once blood glucose climbs past the level the kidneys can hold back, some of that sugar spills into the urine. Bacteria that reach the bladder find a richer place to grow.
- **Your defenses respond more slowly.** After weeks of high glucose, the white blood cells that should fight off bacteria don't work as well. A small infection gets a head start before the body catches up with it.
- **The bladder may not empty completely.** Over the years, diabetes can affect the nerves that control the bladder. Urine left behind after you go becomes a comfortable spot for bacteria to settle.
- **Some treatments play a role.** Certain diabetes medicines work by making the body flush more sugar out through the urine, which can raise the chance of urinary or genital infections. If you take one, please don't stop it on your own: it is a helpful treatment, and any change should be decided during a visit, with your readings on the table.

Women also face more vaginal yeast infections when sugar runs high, and those can easily be mistaken for a bladder infection.

## Quiet signs that are easy to miss

Many people wait for the sharp burning they remember from a past infection. With diabetes, an infection can show up more quietly, especially when nerve changes mean the bladder no longer "speaks up" the way it used to. These clues are worth writing down:

| What you notice | Why it gets overlooked | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose running higher without any change in food or medicine | It gets blamed on diet or stress | Check for urinary discomfort and come in if it lasts several days |
| Getting up more often at night to urinate | It feels like the usual diabetes pattern | Mention it at your next visit, or sooner if anything else shows up |
| Urine that smells different, looks darker or has a pink tint | It gets put down to not drinking enough | Drink more fluids and get a urine test if nothing changes |
| Pressure or heaviness low in the belly, with no burning | It doesn't feel like an infection | Have it checked; no burning doesn't rule anything out |
| Unusual tiredness, mild chills or nausea | It gets confused with a virus | Take your temperature and glucose and don't let more than a couple of days go by |
| Itching or discharge in the genital area | People treat it with store products alone | Get checked, since it may be yeast rather than a bladder infection |

The practical rule is simple: if you have diabetes and something changed in your urine or your readings for no clear reason, don't wait for it to "go away." A quick test clears up the question.

## Lowering your risk day to day

No routine prevents every infection, but these steps make a real difference:

1. **Keep your glucose within the target you were given.** This protects you the most because it goes after the root cause: less sugar in the urine and an immune system that reacts better. Staying current on your A1C tells you whether you're on track.
2. **Drink water throughout the day.** Urinating often helps wash bacteria out before they settle in. Plain water is best; sodas and sweetened juices push glucose up and work against you.
3. **Don't hold it.** Drivers, cooks, cashiers and people caring for a relative often put off the bathroom for hours. Build in breaks even when you don't feel the urge; holding urine is harder on a diabetic bladder.
4. **Give yourself time in the bathroom.** If you feel you haven't fully emptied, wait a moment and try again before getting up. If it happens often, let the medical team know.
5. **Keep intimate hygiene gentle.** Wipe front to back, wash with water and a mild soap, and choose breathable cotton underwear. Douches and scented products irritate more than they clean.
6. **Learn your own early signs.** If you've had a urinary infection before, write down what it felt like. Knowing your pattern helps you get seen in time the next time around.

## Red flags with diabetes: the ER or 911, not the clinic

In someone with diabetes, a urinary infection can travel up to the kidneys and turn serious quickly. Skip the clinic and get emergency care if any of these appear:

- A high temperature with shaking chills and a sharp ache along one side of your waist
- Vomiting that keeps you from holding down fluids or your medicines
- Severe confusion, unusual sleepiness or trouble waking up
- A strong urge to go, yet no urine comes out at all
- Fainting, or strong dizziness when you stand up
- Very high glucose that won't come down with your usual treatment, along with fast breathing or fruity-smelling breath

In any of these situations, go to the emergency room or call 911.

## Questions our patients with diabetes ask

**Can it be an infection if it doesn't burn?** Yes. With diabetes, an infection may cause little discomfort. That's why a urine test is worth doing when your glucose or your urine changes, even without burning.

**Will cranberry juice protect me?** There's no solid evidence that it does, and store-bought juices usually carry a lot of sugar. Plain water is the better ally.

**Should I stop my diabetes medicine because I got an infection?** Not on your own. Bring the list of what you take to your visit, and any change will be decided there.
