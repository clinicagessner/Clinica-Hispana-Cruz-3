Hardly anyone checks their own neck on purpose. The lump usually shows up by accident: in a birthday video where you catch yourself in profile, when the top button of your work shirt suddenly feels tight, or when your sister points at your throat across the kitchen table and asks what that is. From then on it's hard to stop touching it. This piece is written for that moment, to help you sort out what it could be, what a thyroid ultrasound can clear up, and which signs mean you shouldn't sit on it.

## Start by figuring out where the lump sits

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland low in the front of the neck, below the Adam's apple and above the breastbone. One useful clue: because it's attached to the windpipe, the thyroid rides up and down every time you swallow.

You can try a quick check at the bathroom mirror:

1. Grab a glass of water and tip your head back slightly.
2. Take a sip while watching the lower part of your neck on either side of the windpipe.
3. Look for anything that bulges or lifts with the swallow and then settles back.

If the lump sits off to the side, under the jaw or behind the ear, and stays put when you swallow, it probably isn't the thyroid. Many of those are lymph nodes reacting to a throat or tooth infection. That still deserves a look, but it changes the questions the clinic's medical team will ask you.

## Three patients, three different stories

**Rosa, 46, works at a daycare in Westbury.** A coworker mentioned her neck looked "full." It doesn't hurt, but over the past year she's been more tired, feels cold when nobody else does, and has put on weight without eating differently. Here the lump and the symptoms may be linked: the whole gland can enlarge evenly (what many people call a goiter) and at the same time run slow. Her visit needs to answer two things: what the gland looks like, and how well it's working.

**Julián, 33, paints houses.** He felt a chickpea-sized bump on one side while shaving. Otherwise he's fine, no fatigue, no racing heart. Most likely this is a single nodule, and the main question is what it's made of: fluid or solid, how big it is, and whether there are others too small to feel.

**Doña Marta, 62.** Her neck has looked a little wide for years, but lately her collars feel tight, her voice has turned hoarse and pills are harder to get down. What matters here isn't so much the lump itself as the fact that it's changing and is starting to affect swallowing and her voice. That kind of change doesn't wait for a free afternoon.

## What the ultrasound can tell you

Ultrasound uses sound waves instead of radiation, and it's painless. You lie back with a pillow under your shoulders so your neck stretches a bit, a cool gel goes on the skin, and the probe glides over the front and sides of the neck. The scan is quick, with no fasting or special prep. An open-collar top helps, and leave necklaces and chains at home.

From those images it's possible to:

- Measure the gland and see whether it grew evenly or in one spot.
- Tell whether a nodule is solid or filled with fluid, which fingers alone can't sort out.
- Count the nodules, since finding one or two you couldn't feel is common.
- Record a baseline size to compare against at a future check.

At the clinic, staff explain what's on the screen in Spanish. What the ultrasound doesn't do is hand each nodule a final label. Think of it as the first good look, not the last word.

## A fact that may ease your mind

According to MedlinePlus, from the U.S. National Library of Medicine, the vast majority of thyroid nodules are benign. They're very common, especially in women and as people get older, and plenty are found by chance. So the point of checking a lump isn't to scare you; it's to learn its size, what's inside it and whether it needs anything beyond watching.

With the scan and the visit together, the medical team decides the next step. Sometimes that just means measuring it again later. Sometimes it means blood work. And when a nodule has features worth a closer look, the medical team guides your referral to a specialist for a more detailed study.

## Pictures and blood work fill in different blanks

An ultrasound shows how the gland looks, not how it performs. Blood work covers that part. Through the clinic's [thyroid care](/services/tiroides) service, the evaluation includes a neck exam and function tests such as TSH, free T4 and T3, with no fasting and no order from another provider needed. If an autoimmune cause is suspected, the medical team can add antibody testing.

Putting the two together is what gives you a real answer:

| What you notice | What the ultrasound usually clarifies | What blood work usually clarifies |
|---|---|---|
| Neck wider all the way across | Whether the whole gland is enlarged | Whether it's running slow or fast |
| One small bump on one side | Size, and whether it's solid or fluid | Whether function is also off |
| Lump plus fatigue, feeling cold, thinning hair | How the gland looks | Whether it points to hypothyroidism |
| Lump plus palpitations or shaky hands | Nodules or overall growth | Whether it points to hyperthyroidism |

If you've had [blood tests](/services/examenes-sangre) before and someone once mentioned your thyroid was "off," bring that report. Comparing helps.

## When not to wait

Go to the ER or call 911 if a neck lump comes with:

- Trouble breathing or noisy breathing.
- Being unable to swallow saliva or liquids.
- Swelling that grows over hours, with high fever or severe pain.

And get seen soon, rather than letting weeks go by, if:

- The lump has visibly grown in a short time.
- Your voice has gone hoarse and isn't coming back.
- Food or pills feel like they catch on the way down.
- The lump feels hard and doesn't move when you swallow.

On the other hand, if the bump showed up together with a sore throat, fever and tender glands on the sides, an infection is the more likely story. The throat comes first in that case, and a [strep test](/services/prueba-strep) shows whether bacteria are behind it.
