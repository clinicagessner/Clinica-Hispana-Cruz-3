# Diabetes, High Blood Pressure and Flu: What to Do First

October's cooler mornings also bring the start of flu season in Houston. For most people, the flu means a few rough days in bed. But if you live with diabetes, high blood pressure or both, the same virus can turn into something more serious. The goal here isn't to scare you. It's to explain why your body reacts differently and to help you have a plan ready before the first fever shows up.

In this guide, the clinic's medical team walks you through what changes when someone with a chronic condition catches the flu, what to do in the first hours and when it's time to stop waiting.

## Why the flu hits harder when you have diabetes or high blood pressure

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists people with diabetes and heart disease among the groups at higher risk of flu complications, such as pneumonia or a hospital stay. Poorly controlled high blood pressure often goes hand in hand with heart and kidney problems, so it belongs in this conversation too.

Three main reasons explain that extra risk:

- **Infection pushes blood sugar around.** While fighting a virus, your body releases stress hormones that tend to raise glucose, even if you're eating less than usual. At the same time, a poor appetite or vomiting can make it drop. That up-and-down swing is what you need to keep an eye on.
- **Your defenses respond more slowly.** When glucose stays high for a long time, the immune system has a harder time reacting, and the flu can drag on or open the door to a lung infection.
- **Your heart works overtime.** Fever, dehydration and inflammation all make the heart pump harder. For someone with high blood pressure or an already strained heart, that extra load matters more.

| Condition | What the flu can do | What to watch at home |
|---|---|---|
| Diabetes | Glucose higher or lower than usual, dehydration | Check glucose more often, fluids, whether you can eat |
| High blood pressure | Extra strain on the heart, dizziness if dehydrated | Blood pressure readings, pulse, chest pain or shortness of breath |
| Both | Slower recovery and higher risk of complications | All of the above plus how the fever changes |

## Flu symptoms you shouldn't just tough out

Influenza usually comes on suddenly, not gradually like a common cold. Watch for:

- Fever or chills
- Body aches and a strong headache
- Exhaustion that knocks you down
- Dry cough and sore throat
- Stuffy or runny nose

Many patients from Meyerland and Braeswood tell us they waited "to see if it would pass" because they were already used to feeling off from their diabetes. With a chronic condition, that wait is exactly what you want to avoid.

## What to do in the first hours: a step-by-step plan

1. **Check and write it down.** Test your glucose more often than usual and take your blood pressure if you have a home monitor. Log the numbers with the time; that notebook is very useful to the medical team when you come in.
2. **Sip fluids steadily.** Water or broth, in small, frequent sips. Dehydration raises blood sugar and can drop your blood pressure suddenly, which makes you dizzy.
3. **Try to eat something.** Even without an appetite, small portions of something gentle (soup, oatmeal, saltine crackers) help keep glucose from crashing.
4. **Don't stop or change your medications on your own.** Keep taking your treatment as prescribed and, if you're unsure because you can't eat, ask the clinic before making any adjustment.
5. **Be careful with over-the-counter cold medicine.** Some decongestants can raise blood pressure, and certain cough syrups contain sugar. Before buying one, ask which option is right for you.
6. **Come in early, not on day five.** For people with diabetes or heart disease, the CDC advises flu antivirals, and these medicines do the most good when started in the first two days of symptoms. Only the medical team can decide whether they're right for you, which is why an early visit matters.

## When not to wait: ER or 911

Go to the emergency room or call 911 if you or a family member has:

- Trouble breathing or very fast breathing
- Chest pain or pressure
- Bluish lips or nails
- Confusion, unusual drowsiness or trouble waking up
- Very high glucose that won't come down, or low glucose that won't correct, with vomiting or fruity-smelling breath
- Very high blood pressure with a severe headache, blurred vision or weakness on one side of the body
- Vomiting so frequent that even small sips of water or broth come back up
- Fever or cough that improves and then comes back worse

## Prevention before the virus arrives

The best strategy starts before peak season. The CDC recommends a flu vaccine every year for nearly everyone six months and older, and considers it especially important for people with diabetes or heart disease. We offer the [annual flu shot](/services/vacunas) at the clinic; get it during the same visit as your regular checkup.

Besides the vaccine, these habits help a lot:

- Get your diabetes and blood pressure as well controlled as possible before winter. To review the basics of day-to-day control, read our [diabetes management guide](/blog/control-diabetes-houston-guia-pacientes).
- Wash your hands often and avoid touching your face.
- Keep some distance from relatives or coworkers who are sick.

## Your sick-day kit

Put it together today, while you feel well, and keep it somewhere easy to find:

- Glucose meter with spare strips and lancets
- Thermometer and, if you have one, your blood pressure monitor
- A written list of all your medications and when you take them
- Sugar-free drinks and a few gentle food options
- A notebook to log glucose, blood pressure and temperature
- The clinic's number and that of a trusted relative

With this kit ready, you won't have to improvise when the fever starts.
