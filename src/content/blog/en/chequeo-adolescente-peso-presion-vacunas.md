# Your Teen's Checkup: Weight, Blood Pressure and Vaccines

At 14, Daniel walks into the exam room with his cap on, answers every question with "fine" and scrolls his phone while his mom lists what worries her: he barely sleeps, he eats at odd hours, he shot up almost overnight. On the drive home he finally asks what has been on his mind for months: "Is it normal to have lumps on my chest?" Nobody answered it, because he never said it inside.

That scene plays out in a lot of Meyerland families. A teenager's checkup isn't the same as a young child's: the body changes fast, embarrassing questions pile up, and the usual numbers mean something different. Here's what gets checked and how to make the visit count for more than a signed sheet of paper.

## Why the checkup changes between 11 and 18

During adolescence a body can grow several inches in a year, hormones wake up and sleep drifts later into the night, just as your son or daughter starts making their own choices about food and schedules. That's why the AAP's Bright Futures guidelines, the main U.S. reference for children's preventive care, call for a visit every year at this stage, even when the teen feels perfectly well.

If your teen already needs a [school or sports physical](/services/examen-fisico-escolar), use that same visit: it covers vital signs (weight, height and blood pressure), vision and hearing screening, and health recommendations.

## What gets measured and how it's read at this age

| What's checked | Why it matters in the teen years |
|---|---|
| Weight and height | Compared with growth charts by age and sex, not with adult targets |
| Body mass index (BMI) | Read as a percentile; the same number can be normal at 17 and high at 12 |
| Blood pressure | Can rise with no symptoms, especially with extra weight or a family history |
| Vision and hearing | A blurry whiteboard or earbuds at full volume can hurt grades without anyone noticing |
| Vaccine record | Shows which boosters are up to date and which ones to look at |

### Weight: a conversation, not a lecture

A high percentile on the CDC charts isn't a label. It's a starting point to talk about habits: sodas and energy drinks, skipped breakfasts, hours on screens, how much they move outside of school.

One tip for parents: avoid commenting on your teen's body in front of siblings, aunts, uncles or grandparents. At this age a joke about "chubby cheeks" or being "too skinny" lands hard. Watch for the opposite extreme too: skipping meals on purpose, exercising obsessively or hiding food are worth bringing up at the visit.

### Blood pressure isn't only an adult issue

High blood pressure isn't only a grown-up problem: it shows up more and more in teens, especially with extra weight or when a parent or grandparent lives with hypertension. Under the AAP guideline, from age 13 a reading of 130/80 or higher already counts as high.

One reading isn't enough to call it hypertension. Nerves, a morning coffee or the wrong cuff size can push the number up, so it gets repeated and confirmed at later visits. And if you, as a parent, already live with one of those conditions, it's a good moment to check on your own [diabetes, blood pressure and cholesterol care](/services/condiciones-cronicas).

### Is lab work due?

Not every teen needs blood work every year. The AAP suggests checking cholesterol at least once between ages 9 and 11 and again in late adolescence. The American Diabetes Association also recommends screening for type 2 diabetes from age 10 (or the start of puberty) in young people with extra weight and other risk factors, and Hispanic background is one of them. An A1C and a lipid panel from our [blood tests](/services/examenes-sangre) answer the question. Plan on a morning without breakfast for the lipid panel (water is allowed).

## Vaccines: what to look for on the record

Bring the vaccine record, or a photo of it on your phone. The CDC recommends a tetanus, diphtheria and whooping cough booster (Tdap) around age 11 or 12, and a flu vaccine every fall. At our clinic we give the [flu vaccine and the tetanus booster](/services/vacunas); if the record shows another vaccine the CDC recommends for teens is missing, ask at the visit what the next step is.

A practical detail: teens get lightheaded with needles more often than younger kids. Have them eat something beforehand, stay seated during the shot and remain seated for about 15 minutes afterward, as the CDC advises. If your teen has ever fainted with a needle, mention it when you check in.

## The questions that feel too awkward to ask

Almost every teen shows up with a question they won't say in front of family. These are some of the most common, and every one of them has an answer:

- **"Is what's happening to my body normal?"** Breast tissue growth in many boys during puberty, a cracking voice, or a growth spurt that comes earlier or later than friends' are usually a normal part of development.
- **"Why won't my acne go away?"** Some treatments work far better than home remedies. When the medical team starts one, we e-send that prescription to whichever pharmacy your family uses.
- **"Is my period normal?"** In the first few years it's common for it to be irregular. It's worth mentioning if bleeding soaks a pad every hour, lasts more than seven days, or the pain makes her miss school.
- **"I'm tired all the time."** Sometimes it's lack of sleep, and sometimes there's something behind it, such as anemia or a [thyroid](/services/tiroides) problem, which a blood test can clear up.
- **"I can't fall asleep without my phone."** Teens need 8 to 10 hours of sleep, according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine. Moving the phone out of the bedroom at night is one of the changes that shows the quickest results.

### Mood deserves a conversation too

Mood swings come with the territory, but some signs deserve attention: sadness or irritability lasting more than two weeks, pulling away from friends or activities they used to enjoy, grades dropping suddenly, or not eating. Bringing it up at the checkup is a good first step. If your teen talks about hurting themselves, call or text 988 (the crisis line, with help in Spanish), and if there's immediate danger, call 911.

### A few minutes alone

Many guidelines recommend that at some point in the visit, the teen gets to talk for a few minutes without parents in the room. It isn't distrust: it just makes the question they won't ask in front of mom easier to say. If that would help your teen, ask together at check-in how the visit can be organized.

## Preparing for the visit in five minutes

1. **Have your teen type their questions on their phone.** Even if it's two lines, and even if they don't want to show you.
2. **Gather the family history.** Diabetes, high blood pressure, cholesterol, thyroid problems, or a sudden death of someone young in the family, especially if your teen plays sports.
3. **List everything they take.** Including gym protein, supplements and energy drinks, which teens use more than most parents realize.
4. **Agree on who does the talking.** Ideally your teen answers first and you fill in afterward.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does my teen need a checkup if they never get sick?** Yes. High blood pressure, high blood sugar or a vision problem rarely cause symptoms at first, which is exactly why they're checked every year.

**What if something comes back outside the normal range?** The clinic's medical team explains in Spanish what the result means and when it should be repeated.
