# Diabetes and Your Heart: Why High Sugar Raises the Risk

When someone hears they have diabetes, nearly all the attention goes to the glucose meter: the morning reading, which meal pushed it up, whether the pill is working. Yet there's a part of the story that rarely comes up around the family table: the most serious long-term complication of diabetes usually isn't the sugar itself, but the heart and the arteries that feed it.

According to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/diabetes-complications/diabetes-and-your-heart.html), people with diabetes have twice the risk of heart disease compared with people who don't have it. That isn't a verdict, but a reason to look past glucose and check early what truly protects your heart.

## What high sugar does to your arteries

Picture your arteries as flexible hoses, smooth on the inside. When glucose stays above target for months, that inner lining slowly wears down:

- **The walls become inflamed and stiff.** Excess sugar binds to the proteins in your blood vessels and makes them rigid, so the heart has to push harder with every beat.
- **Fat finds a place to settle.** On a damaged wall, cholesterol forms plaque that over the years narrows the path for blood or breaks open into a clot: that's how a heart attack or a stroke happens.
- **Blood gets "stickier."** Diabetes makes platelets clump together more readily, which helps clots form inside an artery that is already narrow.
- **Nerves send weaker warnings.** Long-standing diabetes can damage the nerves that carry pain signals from the heart. That's why some people with diabetes have heart trouble with mild or confusing symptoms, without the classic pain everyone expects.

None of this happens overnight, which is why it can be slowed: between the first damage and a serious event there are usually years in which a few simple checks make a real difference.

## Sugar rarely travels alone

Type 2 diabetes seldom shows up alone, and each companion adds risk for the heart:

- **High blood pressure.** Stiff arteries raise pressure; high pressure injures the arteries further. It's a loop that feeds itself, and it doesn't hurt.
- **Out-of-balance cholesterol.** Diabetes often brings high triglycerides, low "good" cholesterol (HDL) and small LDL particles that slip easily into the vessel wall.
- **Belly fat.** Fat stored around the waist worsens insulin resistance and raises blood pressure.
- **Tired kidneys.** Kidneys and heart look after each other: when the kidneys start leaking protein into the urine, it's often a sign that small blood vessels throughout the body are under strain.
- **Smoking.** If you smoke on top of diabetes, artery damage multiplies.

If you have diabetes, the useful question isn't only "How is my sugar?" but also "How are my blood pressure, my cholesterol and my kidneys?"

## What to check and what each test tells you

This table sums up the checks that say the most about your risk. Through our [diabetes, high blood pressure and cholesterol care](/services/condiciones-cronicas) service, the clinic's medical team goes over these numbers with you and writes down how often to repeat each one.

| What is checked | What it tells you | General target |
|---|---|---|
| Hemoglobin A1C | Your average sugar over the last three months; the best gauge of long-term damage | For many adults, below 7% |
| Blood pressure | How much force your arteries take with each heartbeat | For most, below 130/80 |
| Lipid panel | Total cholesterol, LDL, HDL and triglycerides | LDL goal depends on your risk level |
| Kidney function | Whether the small vessels in the kidney already show wear | Read together with the other values |
| Electrocardiogram | Your heart rhythm and signs of strain or a past problem | The medical team explains the tracing |
| Weight and waist | How belly fat affects insulin and blood pressure | Any steady loss helps |

These targets are a general reference; yours are set according to your age, your other conditions and the medications you already take.

A1C, the lipid panel and kidney function are measured with [blood tests](/services/examenes-sangre); for the lipid panel, 8 to 12 hours without food is recommended (water is fine). An [electrocardiogram](/services/electrocardiograma) records your heart's electrical activity in a few minutes, doesn't hurt, needs no preparation and is advised for people living with high blood pressure, diabetes or high cholesterol.

The kidneys are also monitored with a [urine test](/services/infecciones-urinarias); why urine matters so much with diabetes is covered in [diabetes and urinary infections](/blog/diabetes-infecciones-urinarias-prevencion).

## A heart attack with diabetes may not look like one

This is the point most worth sharing with your family. Because of the nerve damage described above, a person with diabetes can have a serious heart problem without the crushing chest pain seen in movies. Sometimes what shows up instead is:

- Shortness of breath that comes on suddenly, even at rest
- Extreme tiredness or unexplained weakness
- Nausea, cold sweat or dizziness
- Discomfort in the jaw, neck, back or arm
- Chest pressure or heaviness that comes and goes

For severe chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, a face drooping on one side, a weak arm or slurred speech, **call 911**. Don't drive yourself, don't wait to see if it passes, and don't come to the clinic: those signs need an ambulance.

## What you can start this week

Small steps you keep up protect more than a big push that lasts two weeks:

1. **Know your four numbers.** A1C, blood pressure, LDL and kidney function. Save them on your phone with the date they were taken; if you can't remember one, that's the first to check.
2. **Walk after meals.** Ten or fifteen minutes around the block after your main meal helps lower sugar and, over time, blood pressure too.
3. **Look at the whole plate, not just the sugar.** Many people with diabetes watch the bread and the soda but forget the salt in cold cuts, instant noodles and fried snacks, which weigh on blood pressure and cholesterol.
4. **Take your medicines even when you feel fine.** High blood pressure and high cholesterol don't hurt; feeling well doesn't mean you no longer need treatment. If a medicine disagrees with you, talk with the medical team before changing anything.
5. **If you smoke, ask for help quitting.** Your arteries notice this change quickly.

## Frequently asked questions

**If my sugar is under control, is my heart already protected?** It helps a lot, but it isn't enough on its own. Blood pressure and cholesterol need to be on target as well, because each one damages the arteries in its own way.

**Can I stop my blood pressure pill once my readings look good?** Not on your own. Often they look good precisely because you're taking it. Any adjustment is decided at a visit, with your readings in front of you.

**What if the results show something more?** The medical team explains what each value means. If, as general medical guidance, a specialized heart evaluation is needed, you'll be guided on the next step.
